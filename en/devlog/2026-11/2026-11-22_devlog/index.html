Screening Whether to Turn On Thinking Mode for the AI Decision Nodes - Rejected on Pre-Registered Criteria

2026-11-22

I replayed whether giving the three AI roles that make the final call some "time to think" (reasoning tokens) would help. This is how I locked the pass/fail bar before looking at results, and rejected it on output quality rather than profit.

This is the English version of a post originally written in Korean for my algorithmic trading system devlog(new tab).

Does giving the deciding AI "time to think" make it better?

In my system, the final trading decision is made by three AI roles reaching a consensus(new tab).

Today's LLMs support a mode where, before answering, they first spend a stretch of "reasoning tokens" laying out their thinking — so-called thinking mode.

That raises an obvious question. If I turn thinking on for these three decision nodes, do they decide better?

I locked the pass/fail bar before looking at any results

With an experiment like this, if you start setting the bar after seeing the numbers, you can fit almost any conclusion you want.

So this time I registered the pass/fail criteria in a doc up front, before running anything.

There were two rules. One: do not judge it by profit or hit rate. Two: do not change the reasoning-token budget, the target nodes, or the test dates after seeing the results.

The first thing to look at was not "performance" but "does this even run cleanly." Concretely: what fraction of the model's outputs parsed into the required format, and how many answers got cut off mid-thought because the reasoning ran long.

Since this experiment changes the act of judging itself, letting profit catch your eye first makes it easy to rationalize — "it went up a bit this time, so let's wave the quality issue through." Fixing the order of inspection was meant to block exactly that.

Verdict: can't use it — it failed on quality, not on profit

I replayed the thinking variant (T) side by side with the current approach (A) across several days and a few hundred names.

The verdict was: can't use it.

The reason was the quality bar I'd set in advance. With thinking on, the share of outputs that parsed cleanly into the required format fell below the floor I'd drawn.

On top of that, there were dozens of cases where the reasoning spent its whole budget and still wasn't done, so the answer got truncated. That means a whole decision gets thrown away — not a number you can shrug off.

It washed out on operational stability before I ever got to performance.

Why I didn't look at profit

As a side note, turning thinking on did visibly change the decisions. It became far more of an "actor" — leaning toward making trades rather than sitting still — and the per-name wall-clock went up quite a bit, too.

Seeing changes like that makes you want to know "so did it make more money?" But I didn't touch that.

Once decision quality fails to clear the floor, starting to stare at profit figures makes you excuse the quality defect on the strength of a few lucky days of returns. Pinning down "do not re-interpret this by profit" in advance was precisely the guard against that temptation.

So production stays as it is, with thinking off. I've written into the guards that the design I looked at this time — simply bolting thinking onto the current approach — won't be revisited.

I didn't close the door entirely

That said, I didn't lock it shut.

What failed this time was "making it think right inside the decision call that enforces a format." Cramming the thinking and the format-fitting into one call is what produced the truncation and parse failures.

So I left a condition that it can only be revisited with a different design — one that does the thinking in a separate free-text call and keeps the format-fitting call light, as it is now.

Even that can only be looked at after another round of pre-registration and review, and I've pushed any actual production rollout well into the future.

When you try something new, writing down ahead of time what shape you're even allowed to re-examine it in keeps you from stepping into the same trap next time.
