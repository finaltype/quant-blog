A Request to Profit-Simulate the Thinking Mode - The Sample Is Too Small to Decide on Returns

2026-11-23

After the reasoning (thinking) mode failed on output quality yesterday, the next question was "with those changed grades, would I have made more money?" I could have re-run the simulation, but at this scale returns drown in noise, so I decided not to.

This is the English version of a post originally written in Korean for my algorithmic trading system devlog(new tab).

The question that followed yesterday's rejected experiment

In yesterday's post, I screened whether to turn on reasoning (thinking) mode for the three AI roles(new tab) that make the final trading call, and rejected it as "unusable" on output quality rather than profit.

The next question was natural. "I get that the grades changed a lot. But with those changed grades, would I have made more money, or less?"

It's a fair question. So I planned to run a profit-based simulation — and before starting, I checked whether that design even makes sense.

I could have run it

The first thing I confirmed was whether it was feasible at all.

There were ten past nights whose input snapshots line up with the current server setup. I could re-run those ten nights in reasoning mode and reuse the already-saved outputs from the existing mode.

That came to roughly three hours of GPU per night, close to thirty hours for ten. Squeezed into a weekend, avoiding market hours and the nightly batch, it fit.

In other words, I didn't skip it because I couldn't run it. I could run it, and chose not to.

The problem: the profit difference drowns in noise

What stopped me was statistical power.

A portfolio built from a small number of top names swings quite a bit in day-to-day returns whenever the method changes and it picks different stocks. That swing — the noise — is on the order of tens of basis points a day.

The "real skill difference" I actually want to measure is an order of magnitude smaller than that. Even a target like ~10% a year works out to just a few basis points a day.

When a small signal is buried under large noise, pulling it out takes a lot of samples. To statistically distinguish a difference of a few basis points a day, the math said I'd need thousands of trading days.

Ten nights isn't remotely close.

A result where "numbers come out but mean nothing"

So what happens if I run the ten nights? Numbers come out.

Even if reasoning mode has nothing to do with returns, the ten-night cumulative difference lands on some arbitrary value within roughly ±2 percentage points. About one time in three it falls outside even that.

If that number looks good, it can't justify "adopt it"; if it looks bad, it can't justify "reject it." I already know it's a number that can't carry any meaning in the first place.

On top of that, reasoning mode already failed on output quality yesterday. Even a good profit number would be "profit from an unusable method," with nowhere to apply it.

Computing on the existing nights was worse

There was a cheaper option: skip the fresh re-run and just compute returns on the few nights already done in reasoning mode.

That was worse. Those few nights happen to fall inside a holdout window whose read is scheduled for early next year, so peeking at their returns now contaminates that read itself.

It would mean breaking a "don't look at this window until then" rule I had locked in before seeing any results — exactly the kind of move to avoid.

I decided not to, and recorded why in a guard

The conclusion was "no profit simulation."

Instead, I left one line in the decision-guard document explaining why: at this scale, profit can't decide the question, and here are the only conditions under which to revisit it.

And in case the owner later wants numbers just to get a feel — not as a verdict — I pinned down the exact arms, nights, and endpoints to use, now, in the document. That's a guard against reshaping the design to taste after seeing the results.

Why it matters

"Does this make money" is naturally the most tempting question. But not every question gets an answer from a backtest.

When the signal is far smaller than the noise and the sample is short, a profit simulation hands you not an "answer" but a "plausible-looking number." Decide based on that number, and you've effectively based the decision on a coin flip.

This isn't a limitation of this one experiment but a structural limit of the system's scale. So the habit of asking, before running anything, "does this simulation even have the power to resolve what I want to know" saved both thirty hours of GPU and a contaminated read.
